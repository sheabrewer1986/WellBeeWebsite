When our child feels hot, looks flushed or seems unusually unsettled, one of the first things we might reach for is a thermometer.

A raised temperature is common in babies and young children, particularly when they’re fighting an infection. Knowing how to take an accurate reading, what the number means and when to seek medical advice can help you feel better prepared.

In this guide, we'll cover how to take your child's temperature, what counts as a fever, how to care for them at home and when to seek medical advice.

## High temperatures in children

<div class="blogHighlight blogHighlight-yellow">
  <strong>A child's normal body temperature can vary slightly.</strong>
  A temperature of 38°C or above is generally considered a fever.
</div>

A fever is often the body’s natural response to an infection. It is not an illness in itself, but a sign that the immune system is responding to something.

It’s also worth remembering that the number on the thermometer doesn’t tell you everything. How your child looks, behaves, drinks and responds to you can be just as important as their temperature.

A high temperature will usually return to normal within 1 to 4 days.

## How to take your child’s temperature

Having a reliable thermometer at home can be useful when your child is unwell.

A digital thermometer is a reliable and straightforward option for taking your child's temperature. The NHS recommends taking the temperature under the arm for children under five.

**To take a reading:**

- Make sure your child’s armpit is dry.
- Place the tip of the digital thermometer in the middle of their armpit.
- Gently hold their arm against their body to keep the thermometer in place.
- Follow the manufacturer’s instructions and wait until the thermometer signals that the reading is complete.
- Check and record the result.

<div class="blogHighlight blogHighlight-grey">
  When comparing readings, remember to record how the temperature was taken, as readings can vary depending on the measurement method.
</div>

If your child has just had a bath or been wrapped in a blanket, wait a few minutes before taking their temperature, as this can temporarily affect the reading.

For babies and young children, avoid relying on forehead strip thermometers, as they do not provide a reliable measurement of body temperature.

## What should you do when your child has a fever?

Seeing your child with a temperature can be worrying, but many childhood fevers can be managed at home.

The priority is to keep your child comfortable and make sure they’re getting enough fluids.

Here are a few simple ways to help your child feel more comfortable:

### Offer plenty of fluids

Encourage your child to drink regularly. If you’re breastfeeding a baby, continue offering breast milk as normal.

### Look out for signs of dehydration

This includes things such as fewer wet nappies, a dry mouth, sunken eyes or a lack of tears when crying.

### Let them rest

Allow them to rest and don’t worry if their usual routine goes out of the window for a day or two.

### Keep clothing comfortable

Avoid wrapping your child in too many layers, but don’t try to cool them down by removing all their clothing either. Keep them comfortably dressed.

### Keep an eye on their condition

Pay attention to how your child is behaving. Are they responding to you normally? Are they breathing comfortably? Do they seem reasonably alert when awake?

### Check up on them regularly

It's important to continue checking on them throughout the day and night. Be especially mindful of any changes in their behaviour, symptoms or general condition.

## Should you give your child medicine for a fever?

Medicines such as paracetamol or ibuprofen can help relieve discomfort when a child has a fever.

However, the aim isn't necessarily to bring their temperature back to normal. These medicines are generally used when a child is distressed or uncomfortable, rather than simply because the thermometer shows a high reading.

Always check that the medicine is suitable for your child's age and follow the dosage instructions on the packaging or advice from a healthcare professional.

Do not give paracetamol and ibuprofen at the same time. Do not alternate them unless a healthcare professional advises you to.

Ibuprofen may not be suitable for every child. It should not be given to children who are dehydrated, have chickenpox, are under 3 months old or weigh less than 5kg. Children with asthma should only be given ibuprofen if a doctor has recommended it.

Ibuprofen may also be unsuitable for children with certain underlying medical conditions, such as kidney problems. If you're unsure whether it's appropriate for your child, speak to a pharmacist or doctor before giving it.

There are also age restrictions for other medicines. For example, paracetamol should not be given to babies under 2 months old. If you're unsure which medicine is appropriate, seek advice from a healthcare professional.

## When should you seek medical advice?

<div class="blogHighlight blogHighlight-yellow">

<strong>Seek medical advice if your child:</strong>

<ul>
  <li>Is under 3 months old and has a temperature of 38°C or above.</li>
  <li>Is 3 to 6 months old and has a temperature of 39°C or above.</li>
  <li>Is becoming increasingly unwell or behaving unusually.</li>
  <li>Shows signs of dehydration.</li>
  <li>Has a fever lasting five days or longer.</li>
  <li>Develops a rash alongside a fever.</li>
  <li>Is not feeding or drinking adequately.</li>
  <li>Causes you significant concern.</li>
</ul>

</div>

<div class="blogHighlight blogHighlight-red">

<strong>Get urgent medical help immediately if your child:</strong>

- Is extremely agitated, confused or not responding as they normally would.
- Has difficulty breathing or is difficult to wake.
- Has a seizure.
- Develops a stiff neck or sensitivity to light.
- Has blue, grey, unusually pale or blotchy skin or lips.
- Develops a rash that does not fade when pressed firmly with a clear glass.
- Has a swollen or bulging soft spot on their head (in babies).

</div>

If you're in the UK, contact NHS 111 for urgent advice or call 999 in an emergency. If you're elsewhere, contact your local urgent medical service.

## Why keeping track of temperature readings can help

When a child is unwell, it can be surprisingly difficult to remember exactly when their temperature was taken, what the reading was or when they last had medication.

Keeping a simple record can help you build a clearer picture of how your child is doing.

**An example of a simple temperature record:**

| Time | Temperature | Method | Medication given | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8:00 am | 38.2°C | Underarm | Paracetamol | Tired, runny nose |
| 11:00 am | 37.8°C | Underarm | None | Drinking fluids |
| 2:00 pm | 38.5°C | Underarm | Ibuprofen | More unsettled |
| 6:00 pm | 38.1°C | Underarm | None | Resting |

This information can also be useful when speaking to a healthcare professional, particularly if you’re trying to explain how your child’s symptoms have changed.

This is just an illustration of how readings might be recorded. The important thing is to record the information consistently and consider it alongside your child’s symptoms and general condition.
